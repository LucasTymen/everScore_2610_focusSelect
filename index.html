Answer (EN): "Signal flow is the path a signal follows from its source to its destination — for example, from a microphone, through a DSP, into the conferencing platform, and out to the far end. In a meeting room, knowing the signal flow is the basis of any diagnosis: if the far end can't hear the room, I follow the chain — microphone, DSP, codec, network — and I check each stage in order. It turns a mysterious problem into a checklist."

Note: Always think in chains: source → processing → transport → output. An AV incident is located by testing each stage.

Answer (EN): "The most common types are gooseneck microphones on the table, ceiling microphones, and boundary or table-array microphones. Most rooms now use Dante or networked microphones. Typical issues are: no signal (cable, PoE, mute state), low level (gain structure), or poor pickup (wrong microphone selected in the room system). My first checks are always the physical ones: mute, cable, and selection — before touching any configuration."

Note: Types: gooseneck, ceiling, boundary/array — often networked (PoE + Dante). Typical faults: mute, cable, source selection, gain, PoE.

Answer (EN): "In-room speakers are usually powered speakers driven by the DSP or the room processor's amplifier output. Typical failures are: no sound (bad source selection or amplifier channel), low volume (level settings in the DSP), or distortion (clipping, wrong gain structure). I always confirm the signal is present at the DSP output before suspecting the speaker itself — and I check whether the issue affects one speaker or all of them, which immediately tells me if it's a single device or an upstream stage."

Note: Key discriminator: one speaker affected (local) vs all of them (upstream stage: DSP, selection, amplification).

Answer (EN): "Gain is the amplification applied at the input stage, volume is the level at the output stage, and clipping happens when a signal is amplified beyond what the circuit can carry — the top of the waveform is cut off, and it sounds harsh or distorted. In a room, correct gain structure means setting the microphone gain so that normal speech is strong but never clipped, then adjusting the output volume independently. If someone complains about distortion, I check the input gain first — the output is rarely the cause."

Note: Gain = input, volume = output. Distortion almost always comes from excessive input gain, not the final volume.

Answer (EN): "Feedback is a loop: a microphone picks up sound from a speaker in the same room, that signal is amplified again, and it builds up into a howl. Prevention means keeping microphones away from speakers, keeping microphone gain reasonable, and using the DSP's acoustic tools — echo cancellation, noise reduction, and automatic microphone mixing. During a live incident, the fastest fix is to reduce the microphone or speaker level immediately, then restructure the gain."

Note: The loop: microphone → speaker → microphone. Live-incident reflex: lower the level, don't unplug. Prevention: placement, gain, AEC, auto-mixing.

Answer (EN): "AEC is a DSP function that removes the sound coming from the far end out of the signal sent back by the room microphones. Without it, the far-end participants hear themselves with a delay — that's the classic echo complaint. AEC fails or degrades when the delay is too long, when the reference signal is wrong, or when someone disables it. If the far end hears an echo, I check that the correct far-end reference is routed to the AEC block and that no microphone picks up an untamed copy of the audio."

Note: Signature symptom: remote participants hear themselves. Frequent cause: a wrong far-end reference into the AEC block, or an extra microphone outside the DSP.

Answer (EN): "A Digital Signal Processor is the audio brain of the room. It takes all microphone and program inputs, applies processing — gain, equalization, echo cancellation, noise reduction, routing, mixing — and sends the result to the right outputs: the room speakers, the codec, or a recording. In a diagnosis, the DSP is a key stage because every audio path goes through it. I would use its software interface to check meters and routing, or listen to its test signals."

Note: The DSP is the audio brain: inputs → processing (gain, EQ, AEC, routing) → outputs. Diagnose via the software interface's meters and routing.

Answer (EN): "All three carry video and audio. HDMI is the standard in AV; DisplayPort is common on computers and docking stations; USB-C can carry video, audio, data and power on one cable. Typical issues are: bad or unseated connectors, cable length limits, adapters that don't support the required resolution or refresh rate, and dongles that drop the signal intermittently. My reflex is to reseat the cable at both ends, try a known-good cable, and test with a different source to separate the source from the path."

Note: Reflex: reseat connectors, known-good cable, try another source. USB-C: check the adapter actually carries video.

Answer (EN): "EDID is the data block a display provides to describe its capabilities — supported resolutions, refresh rates, audio formats. The source reads the EDID and chooses what to send. When EDID is lost or corrupted — for example through an extension system or a switch — the source may send nothing, a wrong resolution, or fall back to a low default. Symptoms include a black screen with a working source, or wrong resolutions. The fix is usually to re-handshake: re-plug, force the source to redetect, or emulate the EDID at the switch."

Note: EDID = the display's identity card, read by the source. Symptoms: black screen or wrong resolution. Fix: re-handshake, force redetection, EDID emulation at the switch.

Answer (EN): "HDCP is the content-protection handshake used on HDMI and DisplayPort. Every device in the chain — source, switcher, extender, display — must negotiate it. If one element fails the negotiation, typically with mixed firmware or a non-compliant extender, you get a black screen, snow, or an HDCP error message even though the source is fine. In a business environment the content is rarely protected, but the handshake still applies, so I would identify the non-compliant element in the chain and remove or update it."

Note: HDCP: a source-to-display handshake; every link must be compliant. Symptom: black screen/snow despite a working source. Fix: isolate the non-compliant link.

Answer (EN): "My checklist is: first, the physical layer — correct cable type, good condition, properly seated; second, the adapter chain — the fewer adapters the better, and each one must support the resolution and features needed; third, the dock — power status, firmware, and whether the issue follows the dock or the laptop. Note: Order: cable → adapters (keep them few) → dock (power, firmware). An intermittent fault is almost always a connector or cable.

Answer (EN): "A room controller is the touch panel or interface that drives the room — it selects sources, starts and joins meetings, and controls camera, volume and lights. A video conferencing endpoint, or codec, is the device that actually runs the meeting platform and communicates with the far end. The controller is the user's steering wheel; the codec is the engine. Note: Controller = steering wheel (interface), codec/endpoint = engine (platform). A frozen controller doesn't necessarily stop the meeting: different diagnosis.

Answer (EN): "AV over IP means the room's audio, video and control signals travel on a standard network instead of dedicated matrix cables. Sources are encoded onto the network, and displays or processors decode them. Benefits are flexibility and scalability; the trade-off is that AV quality now depends on the network — bandwidth, latency, and multicast configuration. As a support technician, it means my network diagnostics skills apply directly to AV problems."

Note: AVoIP: signals travel on the IP network. Benefit: flexibility. Consequence: AV quality depends on the network (bandwidth, latency, multicast).

Answer (EN): "Power over Ethernet delivers electrical power to a device on the same cable that carries its data. Many AV devices — touch panels, networked microphones, cameras, wireless access points — are powered only by PoE. That has two diagnostic consequences: if a PoE device is completely dead, the first suspects are the switch port, the PoE budget of the switch, and the cable; and a PoE port can be disabled or the cable damaged while the port looks fine. I would check the switch interface for PoE status before replacing the device."

Note: PoE = power + data on one cable. A completely dead AV device: check the switch port (PoE enabled, budget), then the cable, before replacing the device.

Answer (EN): "Latency is the travel time of packets, jitter is the variation of that time, and packet loss is packets that never arrive. In video meetings, latency shows up as delay in the conversation, jitter as choppy or robotic audio, and loss as frozen video or missing words. They often indicate congestion, a Wi-Fi issue, or a QoS problem. In a room with recurrent call-quality complaints, I would check whether the room is wired or wireless, and what the platform's own statistics show for latency, jitter and loss."

Note: Latency = delay, jitter = variation, loss = dropped packets. AV translation: lag / choppy-robotic audio / frozen video. Reflex: platform statistics + wired vs Wi-Fi.

Answer (EN): "QoS, Quality of Service, is the set of network rules that prioritize time-sensitive traffic — typically voice and video — over bulk traffic, so a big file transfer doesn't degrade a call. Multicast is a delivery method where one stream is sent once and received by many devices; it's widely used by AV-over-IP systems, but it requires proper switch configuration. As a technician, I don't need to configure these from scratch, but I need to recognize their symptoms — choppy audio under load, or streams that some devices receive and others don't — and report them accurately to the network team."

Note: QoS = voice/video prioritisation; multicast = one stream for many receivers (common in AVoIP). Posture: recognise symptoms, report precisely to the network team.
